# Playbook

## 1. Pick the structure

| Idea type | Structure | Spine |
|---|---|---|
| A behavior or effect in people ("why do people do X") | **Story-vehicle** | Odd hook. Pick a story the audience already knows. Recap it in 3 sentences. Name the idea it shows. "You will see it everywhere." "The good news: you have one too." Return to the story. Why it works. Paper exercise. Hand back. |
| A belief that holds people back | **Myth-bust** | Striking historical or personal scene. Name the myth. The research that explains it. Who benefits from the myth (usually 2 or 3). The antidote. One picture. An invitation to act. |
| A skill or process the listener must run | **Playbook** | Hook with the cost of not doing it. Name the trap. Numbered steps, each with a short name and a tiny example. Rules of thumb. A tease of the next step. |
| A person, brand, or company that won | **Case breakdown** | Puzzle ("how did X beat Y"). N strategies, each with a name, a one-line definition, and proof. End with a blueprint for the listener's own case. |

Pick one structure per piece. Story-vehicle suits explaining a concept to a general audience. Playbook suits "teach me to do it". Do not mix.

## 2. The nine beats

Before the beats, the insight: a noticed oddity ("that's weird"), one anchor example the listener can inspect, and the question it raises. The beats package an insight. They do not replace it.

1. **Scene first.** A concrete, dated, physical scene, or an odd question. The concept comes after.
2. **Puzzle.** The scene breaks an expectation. Ask the question the listener is already asking.
3. **Name it, define it in one sentence.** A short name, then "X is when...". The name earns its place by mapping parts of the idea, not by sounding good.
4. **Everywhere.** 3 to 6 fast examples from unrelated fields. Then tell the listener they will notice it from now on. Use real examples. If you have none, label them as imagined.
5. **Mechanism.** One reason it works, from how people or groups behave. One paragraph, plain words. It explains the real relationship. It does not restate the metaphor as if the metaphor were the cause.
6. **Open loop.** Promise the how-to early and delay it ("in a second", "more on that later").
7. **Steps and the paper exercise.** 3 to 6 steps. One exercise the listener can do on paper in five minutes.
8. **Chain formula.** One repeatable line: A leads to B leads to C. It is a memory line, not a proof. Write it as a line to remember, not as a claim about how something provably works.
9. **Hand back.** Ask the listener to apply it to their own life. Short sign-off. No recap.

## 3. Delivery devices

Offer these as an optional punchy mode. Use two or three on purpose, not all at once.

- **Contrast-reveal.** Set up the expected thing, land the twist in a short sentence. Best at the end of a paragraph.
- **Repeating-frame list.** Same opening words, a new noun each time, escalating. Three to five items, then the cost.
- **Fake-out then reveal.** Offer the obvious answer, then "but underneath".
- **Signpost question.** "So what was going on?" "But how do you do it?" "But why?" One per section. It pulls the listener forward.
- **Familiar story carries the new idea.** Recap a story everyone knows in 3 sentences, then name the concept it shows.
- **Spatial metaphor.** One place the listener can picture, with parts that map to the steps.
- **Name every part.** Give each stage a short invented name. Named things travel.
- **Fill-in templates.** "I am the only [category] that [position]." "I can't [step] because [excuse]." A sentence frame makes an exercise doable in one minute.
- **Anchor in the familiar.** Tie a new idea to a category the listener knows, then add one fresh twist. A new idea with no familiar anchor does not land.
- **Who benefits.** For a myth, list who gains from it (usually 2 or 3) before you give the antidote. Incentives make a myth feel real.
- **Pre-empt the misread.** One line on what the idea does not mean.
- **Scope line.** When the topic is big, narrow it out loud ("today I am looking at this through one lens").
- **Credibility on demand.** Two sentences of "why listen to me" only at the point the listener would doubt. Not at the start.
- **Self-caught example.** Show yourself failing at the thing. It proves you live it.
- **Specific over general.** A number, a date, a small physical detail. Vague scenes do not stick.
- **Warm bluntness and one joke per beat.** Never stack a joke on the key line.
- **Sign-off.** A mission line and a question for the listener.

## 4. Review rubric (20 points)

Score each beat 0 (missing), 1 (weak), 2 (strong).

| # | Beat | Test |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Scene first | A concrete scene before any concept |
| 2 | Puzzle | The listener's expectation breaks |
| 3 | Name and one-line definition | Short name, "X is when..." |
| 4 | Everywhere | 3 or more examples from different fields |
| 5 | Mechanism | One plain reason it works |
| 6 | Open loop and signposts | Promise delayed, a question at each section end |
| 7 | Steps and exercise | An action doable today, on paper |
| 8 | Chain formula | One line a stranger can repeat |
| 9 | Hand back | Ends on the listener, no recap |
| 10 | Delivery | Two or more devices from section 3, used on purpose |

Bands: 17 to 20 ship. 12 to 16 fix the two lowest beats. Under 12 restart from the structure choice.

Gates (apply after scoring, any score):
- Invented personal story or lived experience: does not ship. Replace with a real one from the user or an explicit hypothetical.
- No inspectable example, only metaphor about the topic: does not ship. Add one.
- A metaphor or chain formula stated as the cause: rewrite the mechanism beat.
- Sentences any other speaker could say: replace with specifics or cut.

Common failures and fixes:
- Concept first, scene later: move the scene up and cut the first paragraph.
- No name: invent one from the listener's own words.
- Exercise missing: add a two-column page or a fill-in sentence.
- Ends on a recap: replace it with a question the listener answers for themselves.
- Everything feels important: cut to one core and park the rest.

## 5. Diagram picks

| Central picture | Use for | Elements |
|---|---|---|
| Slope with start camp and summit | Overcoming a start-blocker | slope, camp, named obstacles, summit, one arrow |
| Axis with a step-off arrow | Differentiation, positioning | line, crowd of dots, one dot leaving |
| Two columns | Contradiction, gap, expectations vs reality | headers, 3 bullets each, circle the clash |
| Chain of arrows | A cause chain | 3 to 4 boxes, labeled arrows |
| 2x2 | Choosing between two dimensions | axes, four labeled cells, one highlighted |

Before drawing, state the relationship the picture shows. If no picture reveals a relationship better than the sentence does, skip the diagram.

## 6. Worked mini example (original)

Idea: "technical debt slows teams down."
- Structure: Story-vehicle.
- Scene: a kitchen where every dish was stacked in the sink "just for tonight" for six months.
- Puzzle: the cook is fast and skilled, yet dinner now takes three hours.
- Name: "kitchen debt". Definition: "Kitchen debt is when shortcuts today become the work you do before you can start tomorrow."
- Everywhere: a codebase, an unanswered inbox, a messy shared drive, a calendar full of recurring meetings nobody remembers creating.
- Chain formula: shortcut leads to clutter, clutter leads to slow starts, slow starts lead to more shortcuts.
- Exercise: list the five shortcuts you took this month and mark which one you will pay back this week.
- Picture: a chain of arrows (shortcut, clutter, slow start, shortcut) drawn as a loop.
